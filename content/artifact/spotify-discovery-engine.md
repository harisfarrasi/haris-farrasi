Spotify is not valuable because it has a large catalog. In streaming, catalog becomes table stakes. The product problem moves from access to navigation.

The user often opens Spotify with incomplete intent. They may know a song, a mood, an artist, a memory, a genre, a social situation, or nothing at all. The product's job is to convert that vague state into playback before the user gets tired of choosing.

This is why Spotify's discovery surfaces matter. Search handles direct intent. Daily Mix handles familiar variety. Discover Weekly handles low-risk exploration. Radio handles continuation. Editorial playlists handle cultural taste. Autoplay handles the fragile moment after the user's last decision ends.

The best part of Spotify is that discovery is not one feature. It is distributed across the session. A user can enter through search, a playlist, home, library, an artist page, a friend's link, a device handoff, or a recommendation. Each entry point carries a different assumption about intent.

The business case is retention. If every competitor has similar music, Spotify must become better at helping the user continue. The more listening history, playlists, saved tracks, device habits, and taste signals accumulate, the more the product becomes personal infrastructure.

The tradeoff is delicate. Too much familiarity makes the product feel stale. Too much novelty makes it feel wrong. A good recommendation system is not simply one that predicts what a user might like. It must understand when the user wants comfort, when they want surprise, and when they do not want to think at all.

There is also a danger in treating all listening as identity. Sleep music, party music, children's songs, focus playlists, and shared devices can pollute the model. Spotify's system has to distinguish durable taste from situational noise.

The sharp PM lens is that Spotify is not selling music. It is selling reduced decision cost. The product wins when the user feels carried from one good choice to the next without losing agency.

The highest taste in Spotify is transition: one song ending, the next song feeling inevitable, the mood staying intact.

Spotify's strongest business asset is the personal graph of taste. A playlist is not just a container; it is a user's saved interpretation of a mood, era, activity, or identity. Liked songs, skips, replays, follows, device use, and playlist edits become a private demand map.

This gives Spotify a different kind of switching cost from pure catalog. A user can move to another app and still find songs, but they cannot easily move years of taste memory, playlist context, and recommendation calibration.

The PM danger is over-automation. If Spotify chooses too much, users may feel passive. If it asks users to choose too much, the product loses its value. The best experience keeps the user in a light partnership with the machine.
