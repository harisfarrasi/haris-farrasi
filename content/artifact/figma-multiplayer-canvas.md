Figma is not important because it put design tools in the browser. That is the implementation detail. The deeper move is that Figma made the design object multiplayer, inspectable, and operational.

Before Figma, design work often moved through files, exports, screenshots, comments in separate tools, and handoff rituals. The design object and the conversation around it were separated. Product managers reacted to images. Engineers inspected approximations. Designers reconciled feedback from everywhere.

Figma made the file a place. A URL could contain the work, the people, the comments, the prototype, the component system, and the handoff surface. That changed who could participate and when. Product could enter earlier. Engineering could inspect without waiting for a package. Stakeholders could comment in context instead of creating parallel conversations.

The product has a strong system logic. Canvas is the creation surface. Multiplayer is the presence layer. Components are the consistency layer. Comments are the feedback layer. Prototype is the experience layer. Dev Mode is the translation layer between design and engineering.

The business case is expansion across functions. A normal design tool sells to designers. Figma sells to the product organization because the design file becomes shared infrastructure. Once a company's components, design system, product decisions, prototypes, and handoff process live inside Figma, switching cost is cultural, not just technical.

The risk is platform sprawl. As Figma expands into whiteboards, slides, AI, dev workflows, and enterprise governance, it must not lose the clarity of the canvas. The creative core has to remain fast and understandable even as the business expands.

The PM lesson is that collaboration is most powerful when it attaches directly to the work object. Figma did not add conversation around design. It made design itself a shared room.

The product's business quality also comes from compressing handoff loss. In many product teams, intent weakens as it travels from PM to design to engineering. Figma reduces that loss by keeping specs, comments, prototypes, components, and visual decisions attached to the same object. The designer's work becomes less like an exported artifact and more like a living source of truth.

The strongest PM metric is not only active designers. It is cross-functional usage: how often engineers inspect, PMs comment, stakeholders review prototypes, and teams reuse components instead of recreating patterns. Figma becomes more defensible when non-designers depend on it without turning it into a messy approval tool.

The risk is that collaboration can become surveillance. If too many people enter too early, creative exploration becomes performative. Figma must support openness without destroying the private mess required for good design.
