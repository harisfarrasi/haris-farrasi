Slack looks simple because the primitive is simple: a message. But the business is not messaging. The business is reducing the cost of organizational context.

Every company slowly develops a memory problem. At five people, context is carried by conversation. At fifty people, context starts hiding in channels, meetings, documents, tickets, and private messages. At five hundred people, the company can know something and still behave as if it does not know it, because the knowledge is not recoverable at the moment of work. That is the real pain Slack touches. It is not "we need chat." It is "we need the organization to remember what it already decided."

The best way to read Slack is as a live archive. A channel is not just a room. It is a decision boundary. `#product-growth`, `#incident-api`, `#launch-pricing`, and `#customer-escalations` are not labels; they are attempts to make work findable. A thread is not just a UI feature; it is a way to keep one argument from flooding the whole room. Search is not a convenience; it is the difference between a company reusing context and paying the same thinking cost again.

This is why Slack is both powerful and dangerous. If the product only optimizes speed, it becomes noise. If it only optimizes structure, it becomes SharePoint with typing indicators. Slack's taste lives in the tension between fluid conversation and future retrieval. It lets people speak quickly, but it has to leave enough metadata behind so the next person can understand what happened.

The subtle product challenge is that the most valuable knowledge in a company is often informal. It does not begin as a polished memo. It begins as "wait, why are we doing this?", "customer X is blocked", "this rollout is risky", "we tried that last quarter", or "this metric is lying." Slack captures those moments before they become documents. That gives the product strategic importance: it owns the pre-document layer of company thought.

But the same strength creates the failure mode. Private DMs destroy institutional memory. Bad channel naming destroys navigation. Notification overload destroys attention. Too many integrations turn the product into an event landfill. A company can adopt Slack and still become less clear if it never develops communication discipline.

The PM lesson is that Slack is not a communication product in the shallow sense. It is an attention and memory system. The quality of the product should be judged by whether it helps a company preserve context without making everyone constantly available.

A strong Slack implementation should reduce repeat questions, shorten incident coordination, make decisions searchable, and make cross-functional work easier to reconstruct. A weak implementation creates the opposite: faster messages, slower understanding.

The business case is that communication is horizontal, but memory is strategic. Once Slack becomes the place where incidents, launches, customer feedback, product debates, GitHub updates, support escalations, and leadership notes pass through, it becomes very hard to remove. The switching cost is not the message history alone. It is the company's habit of thinking in public.

That is high taste in Slack: not colorful emoji, not casual startup energy, not "chat for work." The taste is that the product understands a company is always forgetting itself, and it tries to make forgetting more expensive.
